# The Cookie Business Playbook

Research on the most successful new cookie businesses worldwide (compiled August 2026), the business models behind them, and how to copy those models.

---

## TL;DR — The 7 proven ways to make money in cookies

| # | Model | Best example | Scale achieved | Capital to start |
|---|-------|-------------|----------------|------------------|
| 1 | Franchise flywheel + rotating menu | Crumbl (US) | 1,100+ stores, $1B+ system revenue | $850k–$1.5M per store (franchisee) |
| 2 | Luxury direct-to-consumer "drops" | Last Crumb (US) | 8-figure sales in year one | Low–moderate (e-commerce + production) |
| 3 | Delivery-first, own a daypart | Insomnia Cookies (US) | 300+ stores, $350M valuation | Moderate per store |
| 4 | Content-led cult bakery | Brooki Bakehouse (Australia) | $25M investment for global rollout | One shop + a phone camera |
| 5 | High-footfall kiosk franchise | Ben's Cookies (UK → global) | Stores across 12+ countries | £150k–£475k |
| 6 | CPG / grocery-shelf brand | Sweet Loren's, Partake (US) | 30,000 / 9,000+ retail doors | Moderate–high (co-packing, distribution) |
| 7 | Home-based custom cookies | Thousands of "cookiepreneurs" | $5k/month–six figures/year solo | $300–$550 |

The single most important cross-cutting insight: **none of these companies win because their cookie is dramatically better. They win on a repeatable demand mechanism** — novelty (weekly rotation), scarcity (drops), convenience (late-night delivery), attention (short-form video), location (captive foot traffic), shelf position (allergen-free niche), or personalization (custom orders).

---

## Market context

- The global cookies market was ~**$17.5B in 2025**, projected to reach **$28.1B by 2035** (~5.1% CAGR).
- Fast growth is concentrated in two ends of the market: **indulgent oversized/gourmet cookies** (Crumbl-style) and **"better-for-you"** (gluten-free, vegan, allergen-free, less sugar).
- Cookies are an unusually good small-business product: cheap ingredients (70–85% gross margins are common), short production cycle, high giftability, extremely photogenic (social-media-native), and legal to produce at home in all 50 US states under cottage food laws.

## Deep dives: the companies and how each one makes money

### 1. Crumbl (USA, founded 2017) — the franchise flywheel + weekly rotation

**What they are:** The fastest-growing dessert chain in US history. Two cousins in Logan, Utah opened one store in 2017; the 2026 franchise disclosure document lists **1,101 franchised locations** and the company has passed **$1B in system revenue**.

**The model, piece by piece:**
- **Weekly rotating menu.** ~6–10 flavors at a time, most rotating weekly. This manufactures urgency ("this flavor disappears Sunday"), drives weekly repeat visits, and gives social media a reason to talk about them every single Monday. Same-store sales outperform fixed-menu concepts because every week is a new purchase occasion.
- **Social-first product design.** Oversized cookies, dramatic toppings, the iconic pink box — engineered to be filmed. Weekly flavor-reveal and taste-test videos became a self-sustaining TikTok genre, contributing directly to 700+ store openings between 2018–2025 with minimal paid advertising.
- **Franchising as the growth engine.** Crumbl doesn't pay for stores — franchisees do. Investment per store is roughly **$850k–$1.5M**; average unit volume is ~**$1.3M–$1.7M/year** with average net profits around **$357k** reported across analyzed stores. Crumbl collects royalties on all of it, so corporate scales with almost no capital expenditure.
- **Tech spine.** One app for ordering, delivery, loyalty, and gifting across all stores; open kitchens for theater; simplified operations (cookies only — no fryers, no complex kitchen).

**What to copy:** the demand loop — *novelty on a schedule → social content → visits → franchise demand → royalty income*. Even a single independent shop can copy the weekly-rotation + flavor-reveal mechanic tomorrow.

### 2. Last Crumb (USA, founded 2020) — luxury positioning + artificial scarcity

**What they are:** "The Hermès of cookies." A dozen cookies for **$140** (~$12/cookie), sold only online, only in limited "drops."

**The model:**
- **Sneaker-drop mechanics.** Countdown timers, limited quantities, waitlist-only access. Early drops sold out in seconds with a **200,000-person waitlist** and **8-figure first-year sales** — reportedly **$1M+ in the first 3 months with $0 marketing budget**.
- **Luxury theater end-to-end.** High-fashion photography, minimalist branding, cookies individually wrapped in matte bags with serial numbers, storytelling copy for each flavor, unboxing experience designed for video. The packaging *is* the marketing: viral unboxing content did the advertising for free.
- **Price as positioning.** At $12/cookie the product isn't competing with bakeries — it's competing with flowers, wine, and gift boxes. Corporate gifting and special occasions justify the price.

**What to copy:** scarcity + waitlist + premium unboxing converts a commodity ($2 of ingredients) into a status good. This model needs great branding more than great baking, and it's the most capital-light path to national reach because there are no stores.

### 3. Insomnia Cookies (USA, founded 2003) — own one daypart, deliver into it

**What they are:** Warm-cookie delivery until 1–3 a.m., started in a UPenn dorm room. **250–300+ locations**, sold in 2024 at a **$350M enterprise value** (double its 2018 valuation), with new owners Verlinvest & Mistral targeting **1,800+ bakeries** worldwide.

**The model:**
- **Daypart monopoly.** Nobody else delivers warm dessert at midnight. Owning "late night" means the competition isn't other bakeries — it's nothing.
- **Site selection by customer, not by traffic.** Stores open next to college campuses and dense young-adult neighborhoods; small cheap storefronts because ~most revenue is delivery — the store is closer to a ghost kitchen with a counter.
- **Warm = differentiated.** The product's moat is temperature and timing, not recipe.

**What to copy:** pick an underserved *time* or *occasion* (late night, office birthdays, hotel minibars, hospital gift runs) and build delivery logistics around it. This works in any city on Earth that has late-night delivery apps.

### 4. Brooki Bakehouse (Australia, founded 2022) — the content-led cult bakery

**What they are:** A single Brisbane shop that became a global brand in three years. Founder Brooke Bellamy's "day in the life of a bakery owner" TikToks went viral; the shop now has **2M+ TikTok / 1M+ Instagram followers**, tourist queues around the block, five+ locations, a bestselling cookbook, and in 2025 secured **$25M from UAE developer Arada** to open in Sydney, Dubai and Abu Dhabi.

**The model:**
- **The founder is the media channel.** Behind-the-scenes storytelling (5 a.m. starts, piping boxes, till-count) — not product ads — built parasocial loyalty. The bakery became a destination people fly to.
- **Oversized NYC-style cookies** as the photogenic hero product.
- **Monetize attention in layers:** retail queues → more locations → cookbook → international licensing/investment.

The same pattern powered London's viral cookie shops (**Crème** in Soho/Notting Hill, whose stuffed cookies draw hour-long queues — Time Out calls the phenomenon "baked goods as the new streetwear") — and the wider "hype bakery" wave.

**What to copy:** if you have one shop and no ad budget, your highest-ROI asset is documenting the business itself daily on TikTok/Reels. Queues are content; content makes queues.

### 5. Ben's Cookies (UK, founded 1984 → international 2011+) — the high-footfall kiosk franchise

**What they are:** Oxford's Covered Market stall that became an international chain via small kiosks in premium-footfall locations — malls, train stations, airports — across the UK, Japan, South Korea, Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, the Philippines, and the Gulf (first overseas store: Dubai Airport, 2011). Franchise investment **£150k–£475k**.

**The model:**
- **Tiny format, huge traffic.** A kiosk needs a fraction of a store's rent-per-revenue when placed in a captive-traffic location. Cookies baked on-site in view; the smell is the marketing.
- **Sold warm, priced by weight** — reinforcing "fresh-made" versus packaged.
- **Franchise for international spread** without operating overseas yourself.

**What to copy:** in high-rent cities, a 10–15 m² kiosk in a transit hub or mall can outperform a full bakery. This is the most proven model for Asia and the Middle East, where mall culture dominates.

### 6. Sweet Loren's & Partake (USA) — the grocery-shelf (CPG) model

**What they are:** Packaged cookie/cookie-dough brands that win a *dietary niche* on retail shelves.
- **Sweet Loren's** (founded by a cancer survivor; free of the top 14 allergens): ~**$26M annual sales growing 21%+**, in **30,000+ retailers**, three consecutive years on the Inc. 5000 (451% three-year growth).
- **Partake Foods** (founded by Denise Woodard after her daughter's allergy diagnosis; top-8-allergen-free, vegan): **200% YoY growth** at peak, **9,000+ stores** including Walmart, Target, Whole Foods and Kroger, **$11.5M Series B** raised; grew 8x faster than the overall snacking category.

**The model:**
- **Claim an unclaimed shelf niche.** "Allergen-free that actually tastes good" gave both brands a defensible position no legacy giant (Oreo, Chips Ahoy) occupied.
- **Founder story = brand story**, which wins both press and buyers' hearts at retailers.
- **Co-packing, not bakeries.** Production is outsourced to manufacturers; the company is really a brand + distribution machine.

**What to copy:** if you'd rather build a product company than run shops, pick a dietary niche with passionate underserved buyers (allergen-free, high-protein, halal-certified, diabetic-friendly), outsource production, and fight for retail distribution. Slower and more capital-hungry, but the outcome is a sellable brand asset.

### 7. The home-based "cookiepreneur" (worldwide) — lowest barrier, real income

**What it is:** Custom decorated sugar cookies and small-batch drops sold from a home kitchen. Legal in all 50 US states under cottage food laws (and similar regimes elsewhere), with **$300–$550 startup costs and 70–85% margins**.

**The economics:**
- Custom decorated cookies sell for **$4–$8 each** ($48–$96/dozen); wedding orders routinely hit $300–$500 and corporate orders $1,000+.
- Documented cases of solo bakers reaching **$5k/month within a year and six figures annually** on systems + Instagram presence alone.

**What to copy:** this is the "validate before you invest" model — build an order book and a following from home, then graduate to a kiosk, shop, or drops model with proven demand.

### Bonus model: trend-jacking (Asia's 2025–26 lesson)

South Korean pastry chef Kim Na-ra (Mond Cookie, Gimpo) fused the viral Dubai-chocolate flavor (pistachio + kataifi) with a chewy marshmallow-skinned cookie — the "**Dubai chewy cookie**." It sold **~1.8 million units through a single Korean convenience-store chain within months** and spread to Hong Kong (Yoajung) and the UAE, with even Crumbl launching a copy. Speed matters more than originality: the operators who moved within weeks of the trend captured queues everywhere from Seoul to Dubai.

## The patterns to copy (what actually makes the money)

1. **Engineer a repeat-visit mechanism.** Weekly rotation (Crumbl), drops (Last Crumb), late-night cravings (Insomnia). Never rely on "people liked it, they'll come back."
2. **Design the product for the camera, not just the mouth.** Oversized, stuffed, gooey cross-sections, dramatic boxes. Every winner above is visually extreme; virality is a product-design decision.
3. **Short-form video is the only marketing channel that matters at the start.** Crumbl, Brooki, Crème, Last Crumb and the Dubai chewy cookie all reached millions with ~$0 ad spend. Post daily; show process, not ads.
4. **Charge more than feels comfortable.** $5–$12 per cookie is proven at scale. Premium pricing funds premium packaging, which funds virality. Nobody on this list competes on price.
5. **Keep the menu tiny and operations simple.** Cookies only. One oven type, no fryers, minimal skilled labor — that's what makes both franchising and solo operation work.
6. **Scale with other people's money once the unit works:** franchisees (Crumbl, Ben's, Dirty Dough, Cookie Plug), investors (Chip City took $17.5M from Danny Meyer's Enlightened Hospitality; Brooki took $25M from Arada), or retailers' shelves (Sweet Loren's, Partake).
7. **Own an occasion.** Late night, gifting, office catering, tourist destination, celebration centerpiece. Occasions have budgets; snacks don't.

## Which model should you copy?

| Your situation | Copy this model | First move |
|----------------|----------------|------------|
| < $1,000, testing the waters | Home-based custom/cottage (#7) | Register under local cottage/home-food rules; open Instagram order book |
| $10k–$50k, want a real brand fast | Luxury DTC drops (#2) or content-led bakery-lite (#4) | Nail 4–6 recipes, invest heavily in packaging + film every batch |
| $100k–$400k, want a shop | Kiosk in high-footfall location (#5) or challenger franchise — Cookie Plug runs **$94k–$158k**, Dirty Dough **$162k–$428k** (or ~$40k mobile unit) | Secure the location before anything else; foot traffic decides everything |
| $850k+, want proven returns | Crumbl franchise (#1) — ~$1.3M AUV, ~$357k avg net profit | Apply through the FDD process; expect saturation in mature US markets, better odds in newer territories |
| Want a sellable company, not a job | CPG niche brand (#6) | Pick an underserved dietary niche, find a co-packer, pitch regional grocers |
| Already have a shop that's flat | Bolt on the mechanics: weekly rotation + daily behind-the-scenes video + late-night delivery window | Costs almost nothing; these are the three highest-ROI retrofits |

**A realistic composite for a new entrant with modest capital:** start home-based or with one small shop → oversized/stuffed signature cookie + 4 weekly-rotating flavors → film everything daily → add drops for holidays (Last Crumb mechanics) → once queues form, choose your scale path (more locations, franchising, or packaged product).

## Niche spotlight: hospital-friendly cookies & "people who can't eat normal cookies anymore"

This niche combines two models from above — **occasion ownership** (the get-well visit, like Insomnia owns late night) and **dietary-niche CPG** (like Partake owns allergen-free) — and it is only partially claimed.

### The rules of giving food in hospitals (what makes a cookie "hospital-appropriate")

1. **Always ask the nurses' station first.** Patients may be NPO (nothing by mouth before surgery), on fluid/texture restrictions, or on condition-specific diets. This is the non-negotiable step.
2. **Commercially sealed beats home-baked.** For immunocompromised/neutropenic patients (chemo, transplant), hospitals commonly steer toward **commercially packaged food** to minimize infection risk — home-baked goods are often turned away. Individually wrapped, shelf-stable, sealed portions are the gold standard.
3. **Label everything.** Full ingredient/allergen lists and per-cookie nutrition (especially **carbs per cookie**, so diabetic patients can dose insulin) make a cookie giftable in a medical setting.
4. **Soft wins.** Post-surgery patients, chemo patients with mouth sores, and elderly patients all favor soft-baked textures.

### Who already serves this market, by condition

| Condition / need | What works | Who's doing it |
|---|---|---|
| Get-well gifting (general) | Comfort-food gift box delivered to home or hospital, personalized note | **Spoonful of Comfort** (US) — soup + rolls + cookies packages, plus a corporate gifting arm and healthcare-worker appreciation boxes |
| Diabetes / low-sugar | Monk fruit & allulose sweeteners, 0–3g sugar, per-cookie carb counts | **ChipMonk Baking** (Houston) — founded after co-founder's Type 2 diabetes diagnosis; DTC from a dedicated gluten-free bakery |
| Celiac / food allergies | Top-9/14 allergen-free, sealed singles | **Partake**, **Sweet Loren's** (profiled above) |
| Chewing/swallowing difficulty (dysphagia — stroke, ALS, elderly, post-surgery) | IDDSI-compliant "transitional foods" that melt with saliva | **Savorease** "Crispy Melts" (US) — first meltable solid snack, sold into hospitals as floor stock and therapy tools; also Blossom Foods, Hormel Health Labs |
| Elderly care (mass market) | Texture-graded foods under Japan's 4-level **Universal Design Food (UDF)** standard ("easy to chew" → "no chewing required") | Kewpie's *Yasashii Kondate* line and many others — Japan is the world's most mature soft-food market and the template to study |
| Chemo taste changes & nausea | **Real ginger** (clinically shown to ease chemo nausea — flavoring alone doesn't count), lemon, mint, cinnamon, maple, almond — flavors that cut through "chemo mouth" (50–75% of chemo patients get taste changes); lemon + maple counters metallic taste | **Nobody owns this.** Cancer centers (Dana-Farber, City of Hope, Fred Hutch) publish the guidance; no branded "chemo-friendly cookie" exists at scale |
| Kidney disease / dialysis | Low sodium, low potassium, low phosphorus — plain shortbread/wafer-style cookies qualify; chocolate and nuts generally don't | **Nobody owns this** either — dietitian blogs and recipe sites fill the void |
| Hospital staff gifting | Individually wrapped, medical-themed tins | Apple Cookie & Chocolate Co.; generic wholesale (Otis Spunkmeyer/Famous Amos snack packs) stock hospital gift shops |

### The opportunity, if you want to build here

- **The two open lanes are chemo-friendly and renal-friendly treats.** Both have desperate, underserved, easily-reached audiences (oncology and dialysis centers are dense, recurring communities), clear evidence-based product specs (real ginger/lemon/soft for chemo; low Na/K/phos for renal), and zero dominant brands.
- **The wedge product:** a "hospital-safe" gift box — factory-sealed, individually wrapped, dietitian-reviewed cookies where the buyer picks the recipient's restriction (diabetic / gluten-free / renal / soft-texture / chemo-comfort) at checkout. Nobody offers this today; Spoonful of Comfort proves the gifting occasion pays, Partake proves the dietary niche pays.
- **Distribution stack:** DTC gifting site (people buy from home to send to hospitals) → hospital gift shop wholesale (individually-wrapped is already the standard there) → corporate HR accounts (get-well/sympathy gifting is a line item) → oncology/dialysis clinic partnerships.
- **Trust is the moat:** dietitian endorsement, per-cookie nutrition panels, sealed single-serves, and testimonials from patient communities matter more here than in any other cookie segment — and once earned, they're hard for a generic bakery to copy.
- **Caveat to design around:** individual patients' restrictions vary and can be strict — every product page and box should say "check with the care team," which doubles as a credibility signal.

---

## One legal warning: copy the model, not the brand

Business *models* — rotating menus, franchising, drops, late-night delivery — are not protectable and are free to copy. Brand *identity* is protectable: Crumbl has sued look-alike competitors (including Dirty Dough and Crave) over trade dress — packaging, box design, presentation style. Use these companies as blueprints for mechanics and unit economics, but build distinct names, colors, packaging, and recipes.
